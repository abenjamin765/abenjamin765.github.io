# Portfolio design research

Date viewed: 19 October 2026
Owner: scout
Status: draft evidence for the newsite intro, navigation, and work panels

## Decision this is meant to inform

What should the first screen of Aaron Benjamin’s portfolio say and show, and how should the rest of the site introduce work, so a design director can tell, quickly, what kind of product designer this is?

This is a review of other portfolios, not research with hiring managers. It can show what curated designers put on the first screen. It cannot show what gets someone hired.

## Questions

1. What does [Wall of Portfolios](https://www.wallofportfolios.in/?company=All) treat as worth featuring?
2. What do those designers put in the hero, the navigation, and the work list?
3. Which of those patterns fit a staff-level product portfolio about complex, shipped work, and which are properties of a different kind of site?

## Method

Reviewed the Wall of Portfolios homepage on 19 October 2026, including the September 2026 “Portfolios of the month” blurbs, the category counts, and the first page of the designer directory. The `?company=All` query did not return a company-filtered list. The page still showed the default directory, and an in-page search state read “No exact matches.”

Opened nine profile pages. Each embeds the designer’s live site in an iframe. Then read the live homepage of each embedded site: title, description, headings, navigation, and the first screen of body text.

## What the directory itself shows

Observations from [the homepage](https://www.wallofportfolios.in/?company=All):

- The site’s own headline is “Discover work from the world’s top designers.” Under it, a logo row and the claim “Trusted by 50,000+ designers from” Netflix, Google, Apple, Uber, Swiggy, CRED, and Microsoft. Those are source claims, not verified reach.
- It sorts portfolios into visual categories, and prints these counts: Minimalist 850+, Dark themed 400+, Interactive 320+, Creative 200+, Modern layout 90+. The catalog’s primary browse model is look, then role, years, country, company, and “open to work.”
- A directory card shows name, country, role, a style tag (Interactive, Creative, Dark Theme, Minimal), years of experience, and company logos.
- September 2026 editorial blurbs praise a repeated pair: a distinctive visual identity, and a short explanation of complex work. The blurbs specifically name playful identity around Microsoft AI and platform work, concise storytelling of large consumer products, a clean visual system with clear project framing, business impact tied to decisions, and motion treated as part of the product rather than polish.
- Profile pages are a thin frame around the live site: photo, name, role, country, years, company marks, then an iframe. A welcome overlay on those pages says “Only 30% of portfolios make it to the Wall.” That is a marketing claim on the profile, not an audited acceptance rate.
- The case-study index ([wall of case studies](https://www.wallofportfolios.in/wall-of-case-studies/)) titles projects by product and problem (“Designing Dovetail for AI-Native Customer Intelligence,” “Visa — Buyer Support / Payments Platform”) and tags some as B2C or B2B.

Inference: recruiters using this catalog can filter for “minimal” or “dark” before they read a case. A distinctive first screen is how a portfolio gets sorted. It is not a substitute for the work list underneath it.

### Work-row pattern

The clearest repeated unit is Dharmesh’s. Each row is:

1. Company and year, small.
2. A short project name.
3. One sentence that says what changed for a person.

Examples from that page: “Meals under your budget / Built a way for budget-conscious users to browse meals that are guaranteed to land within a set price.” “Easier kitchen supply / Redesigned browsing and bulk pricing to help restaurants stock up, boosting order value.”

Niklas uses the same unit without the year: a product name and a sentence about what the work became. Vaibhav adds a metric under the sentence. Aditya makes the title itself the problem.

None of the product-design homepages in this sample open with a process diagram, a tool logo row, or a paragraph résumé. Résumés are a link. About is a later, shorter block. Contact is an invitation in one line (“Your turn. Say hello.” on Dharmesh’s site).

## Synthesis

The portfolios this catalog features are simple in structure and specific in the first two sentences.

The first screen does three jobs:

1. Say who it is and the current or most recent role.
2. Say the kind of designer in one claim.
3. Get out of the way so the work can start.

The claim is where seniority shows up. Earlier-career consumer portfolios say “I design clear, intentional products” or “I move numbers.” The staff examples say how they work: pixels and code, systems, decisive iteration, making a complicated surface understandable. They still do not spend the hero on a biography.

The work list is the argument. A strong row can be read without opening the case: where it happened, what it was, and what became easier, faster, or more honest. Numbers help when they are attached to that row. They are not required for the row to work. Dharmesh’s featured portfolio is praised for concise storytelling and does not lead with percentages.

Visual identity is the other half of what the curators praise. Eemon is featured for a playful system wrapped around Microsoft platform work. Aditya is featured for a clean system that stays personal. The identity is the frame. The rows inside it stay scannable.

Wall of Portfolios will also sort a site into Minimalist, Dark, Interactive, Creative, or Modern before a person reads it. That is a property of the catalog. It is a weak reason to pick a theme. The featured set includes more than one of those looks, and the blurbs talk about clarity and identity, not about which category won.

## Contradictions

- The catalog’s largest category is minimalist, and the September blurbs also reward playful and metric-heavy sites. “Be minimal” and “have a strong identity” are both present. They are not the same instruction.
- Vaibhav’s site argues that the hero should promise measurable impact. Dharmesh’s and Niklas’s sites argue that a precise sentence is enough until the case. Both got onto the wall.
- Shahi and Rishabh spend the homepage on notes, leadership, and range. The product-design features spend it on shipped projects. For an individual-contributor portfolio, the second pattern is the one that matches the featured product designers.
- Rashmi’s skill marquee works for a motion reel. It is the opposite of the product-design pattern, where tools are absent from the hero.
- Directory tags and live copy sometimes disagree. Niklas is tagged Dark Theme on the wall and the homepage copy is about pixels, code, and case studies. Shahi is tagged Minimal and filed as senior, while the site says staff product designer at Deel. Treat the live page as the design evidence and the wall tag as a catalog label.

## Confidence and limitations

Confidence is moderate for “what do featured portfolio homepages do,” and low for “what should Aaron’s site do to be hired.”

Limits:

- Nine sites, chosen because they were featured or on the first directory screen. Not a random sample, and not a set of US staff or principal product designers.
- Homepage text only. Case-study interiors were not reviewed.
- Eemon’s hero sentence appears to animate; the ending was not captured.
- Wall of Portfolios counts, the “50,000+” line, and the “30%” line are claims printed by the site.
- No hiring-manager interviews. A pattern can be common on a portfolio gallery and still be the wrong emphasis for a staff loop.

## Implications for this site

These are inferences from the evidence above, checked against the portfolio already in `src/index.pug` and the panel structure in `newsite/index.html`.

The current production hero already has the right claim: helping teams make sense of complex problems, then shipping something people can use. The newsite intro is a full-viewport yellow panel, the word “Hello,” a large word, and a waving portrait. That panel is the identity. The sample says the identity should be one screen, and the next thing the page does should be the work.

Use the sample this way:

- Keep the hello and the portrait. Add the name, the role, and one claim in that panel. The claim should say what happens after the problem is understood: a product people can act on. Company names can sit in one line. A rotating list of adjectives does not appear in this sample, and it would compete with the portrait.
- Let the intro panel stop there. Experience, about, and a long biography are separate panels already. The sampled sites put those later, and they keep them short.
- On the work panel, use the row these sites repeat. Company and year, a title that names the situation, one sentence, and one outcome. The existing case hooks already fit: “Eight finished. Which two needed help?” and “The wait was short. The uncertainty was not.” Put the outcome on the row (“Shipped in six weeks,” “50-store pilot”) the way Vaibhav puts a number on the card, without making the number the title.
- Keep the navigation to the destinations that these sites actually use. Intro, Work, About, and Contact match the sample. Experience is the extra. If it stays, it should be the résumé, not a second version of the work stories.
- Put personality in the yellow field, the portrait, and the voice of the claim. Keep the work rows typographically quiet so they can be scanned. That is the split the featured sites make.
- Skip a tool list, a skill marquee, a process diagram, and an “open to work” badge in the hero. Open-to-work is a directory filter on the wall, and on the live sites it is a small status, not the headline.

What this evidence cannot decide: palette, type, motion, or whether the panels should scroll or snap. Those are design choices. The sample only says the first screen should be identifiable in one glance and specific in one sentence, and that the work should be the next thing a visitor can read.

## Next research step

Read two case-study interiors from this set (Dharmesh and Niklas, or Vaibhav) and compare their section order with one existing story, such as classroom assignments or curbside pickup. The open question is how much process a staff-level story shows before the decision and the outcome. This homepage pass does not answer that.
