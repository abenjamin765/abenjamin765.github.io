# Titles that name the situation outperform neutral labels

**Claim:** The report says outcome-oriented project titles beat neutral labels, because the reviewer can form a hypothesis before opening the case.

**Sources:** Winning-portfolios report (attachment `deep-research-report__10_.md`); homepage examples in `newsite/research.md`.

## Evidence

**Observed (report):** “Selling more used cars with a video-first detail page” is the report’s example of a title that communicates a problem, an intervention, and a commercial context. “Meals under your budget” is followed immediately by what was built and whom it served. Rishabh tells readers that each of his five cards contains “what changed.”

**Observed (homepage notes):** Aditya’s projects are titled as problems: “Designing for category discoverability,” “Making an invisible price feel honest.” Niklas’s titles name the product change: “Evolved LottieFiles’ web presence…,” “Turned fragmented implementation guidance into a context-aware handoff….” Eemon’s entries are labeled by craft and year: “Researcher agent on the taskbar / Product design & coding — 2025,” “A scalable notification design system / System design — 2026.”

**Observed (homepage notes, catalog):** The Wall’s case-study index titles projects by product and problem, for example “Designing Dovetail for AI-Native Customer Intelligence” and “Visa — Buyer Support / Payments Platform,” and tags some as B2C or B2B.

**Inferred (report):** This works because the reviewer can hypothesize the candidate’s value before investing in the case. The report’s P0 check is: cards communicate more than project or company names.

## Implication for this portfolio

On the work panel, a title can name the situation. The homepage notes say not to make the metric the title. Put the outcome on the row beside it.

## Limit

“Outperform” is the report’s judgment. There is no click-through or recall test in either source.
