Shapes / Product System framework

A design system for more than the interface.

A Product becomes unavailable. Which interfaces change, which actions are allowed, and what happens to the purchase flow?

Shapes connects the definitions, interfaces, and rules a team needs to answer those questions.

My contribution
Defined the reference model, the documentation-to-standard boundary, and the authoring workflow.
Design focus
A connected Product System for designers and engineers, starting with a retail domain.
Current stage
In development

A rule changes.
The team needs the whole picture.

Consider a designer or engineer changing how unavailable products behave. The Product Card is one place to look. The merchant catalog, cart, and purchase flow may depend on the same rule.

A component library explains how the card is built. The definition of availability, the conditions for adding an item to a cart, and the route a shopper takes next can live in separate documents. The team has to reconnect that knowledge to make the change.

I designed Shapes around that connection: a reference that explains the product beneath the interface and makes the relationships between its parts navigable.

Define the Product once.
Explain each context.

I separated an object’s definition from its representations. The shopper card, merchant row, and cart summary point to the same Product, while each explains the information and actions its audience needs.

Keeping a separate definition inside every screen document would make each page self-contained. A shared definition makes the relationship explicit and gives changes a common point of reference. The trade-off is deliberate: contributors have to maintain the links as the product evolves.

The model also needs boundaries. A current catalog price and a recorded cart-line price can have different rules. Cart quantity belongs to a Cart Line. Sharing a definition should clarify those differences rather than erase them.

I organized the framework into objects, actions, representations, surfaces, flows, and UI elements. Object-Oriented UX informs the model: understand the meaningful things, their relationships, and the actions people take on them.

Follow the rule.
See what it means for the interface.

The workspace turns those connections into a reading path. Start with Product, open its Product Card representation, and see how availability shapes both the interface and the next action.

I kept one library tree and one focused document. Attributes and connected references use consistent tables, so the reader can move from a definition to its context without reconstructing a new layout.

The unavailable state now has a complete explanation: the card communicates availability, Add to cart requires an available Product, and the purchase flow gives the shopper another route. The value is in following those relationships, not simply listing them.

Document what exists.
Choose what becomes a standard.

I made documentation, reuse, and enforcement separate decisions. An implementation records what exists. A pattern explains an approach that can repeat. A standard records what an organization has chosen to require.

Automatically promoting every documented interface would make consistency easier to enforce, but it would also flatten differences that serve different audiences or tasks. Keeping these levels distinct preserves the context behind a variation. It also asks the team to make an explicit adoption decision.

Review the meaning.
Then add it to the shared model.

I made contributor review part of authoring. A screenshot can reveal a field or control; source code, requirements, or an owner can explain its permissions and effects. A suggested interpretation becomes a shared definition through that review.

This adds effort compared with accepting extracted content immediately. The payoff is a model whose definitions have a reason to be trusted—and a visible place for questions that still need an answer.

That same discipline supports maintenance. Each definition needs a source, an accountable owner, and a reason to be revisited when the product changes. The framework uses structured, version-controlled definitions linked to implementation and design evidence.

Keeping Product.availability current

Source

A proposed change brings the affected references into review before the shared definition is updated.

I scoped the first release around one complete authoring path: document an interface, model its meaning, connect its context, and publish the reference. Keeping that path focused gives the team something useful to maintain before adding advanced extraction or visual editing.

What I’m testing next

Make understanding faster.
Keep the upkeep small.

The next step is to compare existing documentation with a connected retail reference. Designers and engineers will explain the same Product Card and trace the same availability change in each approach. I’ll compare the answers against a defined set of dependencies.

  1. Understand the feature

    Measure how accurately someone explains the card’s content, actions, and underlying rules, and how long it takes to find the answer.

  2. Trace the change

    Check whether they find the affected representations, action conditions, and purchase-flow states. Record the dependencies they miss.

  3. Maintain the reference

    Have a contributor update the rule and its connections. Compare the effort with maintaining the existing documents and record any definitions left out of sync.

The principle behind Shapes

A useful design system shouldn’t stop at showing us the parts. It should help us understand the product they make.

Explore the six reference types

Objects

What is this thing?

Product identity, attributes, relationships, and lifecycle.

Product → name, price, availability

Actions

What can change?

Who may act, under which conditions, and what happens afterward.

Add to cart → create or update a cart line

Representations

What matters here?

The information and controls an audience needs in a particular context.

Shopper card → name, image, price, add

Surfaces

Where does it live?

How pages, forms, dialogs, and collections compose an experience.

Search results → collection of Product Cards

Flows

How does someone reach a goal?

The sequence of surfaces, decisions, states, and outcomes.

Purchase → choose, review, checkout

UI elements

How is the interface built?

The components, tokens, and implementation details that give it form.

Card + button + typography + spacing
